Part 0 — Strategy

Pick Your Game

The eight voices in this Bible teach you how to make a short-form video that works. Kane Callaway teaches the question that comes before the video: which game are you even playing? Skill is downstream of that choice. You can rep for a year in the wrong game and win nothing — not because your hooks were weak, but because the whole board was wrong. [synthesis] So this Part sits ahead of Foundations: pick the game, and everything in the craft chapters is aimed at the right target.

Callaway’s framing for why the choice outranks the tactics: strategy is commoditized the moment you know it; the real edge is reps, and the distance of each iteration off the last one. Kallaway ▶ A rep only compounds if it is a rep in the right game — which is why picking the board comes first.

Kallaway ▶ The tell is the creator who posts “7 million views a month and I still live in my mom’s basement.” Kallaway ▶ The two product games sell a consumer item — commoditized (show the couch, it sells itself) or education-required (“is that matcha or greens?” needs a sentence of teaching). Pick the game from your business model before you touch craft. This is what dissolves the corpus’s wide-vs-narrow argument (Ch 3) and its “are info products dead” argument (Ch 15) — those were never contradictions, just different games.

0.2 Content Minutes

If §0.1 tells you which game, this tells you the physics common to all of them: trust is bought in minutes watched. To trust you enough to buy, a viewer has to watch a certain number of minutes of your content. Picture each minute as a block — and a block only counts if it is actually watched. Kallaway ▶

≈ 4 blocks → an email lead magnet
≈ 12 blocks → a $97 product
… ≈ 400
≈ 400 blocks → a $10,000 offer
Formula — trust accrual
TRUST = Σ minutes actually watched
1 long-form rep ≈ 20 shorts (in trust)
A 25-minute video at ~9-minute average view ≈ 10 blocks per rep; a 60-second short at ~30-second average ≈ half a block. This is why bingeability matters — a numbered series, or profile covers that tell a binger what each video is — and why Part V (long-form) earns its place: short form feeds the block engine, long form fills it fastest.
“The only way the blocks count is if someone actually watches it.” — Kane Callaway Kallaway ▶

0.3 Traffic, Funnels, Offers

Underneath the five games is the simplest possible model of a business: traffic, funnels, offers — that is the whole thing. Kallaway ▶ You either start from an offer you know you want to sell and go get traffic to funnel into it, or you start from traffic and figure out the offer later (the path Callaway took). Whichever end you start from, the other two still have to exist.

Trafficattention you can generate on demand
→
Funnelthe path from viewer to email to buyer
→
Offerthe thing money is exchanged for

His ranking of the three for a beginner: traffic is the most valuable, because you can have traffic and monetize without an owned offer (brand deals, affiliate), but you cannot monetize an offer without traffic. So if you build one hard skill first, build the ability to generate attention. Kallaway ▶

0.4 Two Buckets: Creator vs Entrepreneur-Using-Content

Before the tactics, decide which person you are. The creator plays the media side — wants influence, reach, to be at the parties, to be known; the media game is the fastest route to that, but it is a hard road, and breaking into seven figures as a pure creator is a top-1%-of-1% outcome. Kallaway ▶ The entrepreneur-using-content treats content as a cheat-code lever to fuel a business and does not need to be famous. Callaway puts himself squarely in the second bucket: “a savage entrepreneur that identified organic content as the cheat-code lever to fuel businesses.” Kallaway ▶ Self-select honestly, because the two buckets run different playbooks.

0.5 Get the Skill First

Starting from zero — no skills, no audience, no offer — the first move is not to make content. It is to build a valuable skill someone will pay for. Kallaway ▶ The strongest starting skills are the attention-generating ones (organic, paid, or written), but product and sales count too — pick one bucket of traffic/funnels/offers and get good. What does “get good” mean? Do the thing and get the result you would want to charge others to help them get. Kallaway ▶ And there is a chicken-and-egg trap: you cannot grow a “how to grow content” channel without having grown content, so learn the craft in a neutral niche first (Callaway picked tech and AI, where the brand deals were lucrative). His blunt corollary: don’t give content advice until you’ve actually mastered content. Kallaway ▶

Do this

  • Name your game before your pillars — which of the five are you selling into? Kallaway ▶
  • Set the block target for your actual offer (lead magnet vs $97 vs high-ticket), then design for bingeability so you stack blocks in one session. Kallaway ▶
  • Write your business as traffic → funnel → offer, and name the one hard skill you’re building first. Kallaway ▶
  • Decide your bucket: creator (media, be known) or entrepreneur-using-content (lever, stay behind it). Kallaway ▶
  • If you’re starting from zero, prove a skill in a neutral niche before you try to sell teaching it. Kallaway ▶

Chapter 1. The Inner Game: Fear, Ego & the Psychology of Posting

The single largest body of teaching in the corpus is not hooks or algorithms — it is the creator's own head. All eight voices treat psychology as load-bearing: the thing that stops most people is never gear or strategy, it is the fear of being seen, the ego's reaction to flops, and the inability to keep going. Chapter 19. The Packaging Game (YouTube)

19.1 Short form is served; long form is chosen

The core difference between the two mediums: on short form the feed serves the video and the viewer has no choice — the hook is the package. On long form the viewer must choose to click, so packaging (the idea, the title, the thumbnail) is a separate and decisive game. If they don’t click, the video might as well have been a black screen; all the effort inside it is worth nothing. Kallaway ▶ Mr Beast’s early lesson: over-invest in packaging or the money spent on the video is wasted. Kallaway ▶

The core split
Short form = served (the hook is the package)  |  Long form = chosen (packaging decides)

19.2 Title and thumbnail first, not last

The most common mistake is doing the packaging at the end, burnt out after three days of editing — and editing is the least important part. Do the title and thumbnail first: you have to confirm the click, and you should spend your freshest effort on the most important thing. If you can’t solve the title and thumbnail, the video is already a dud. Kallaway ▶

19.3 The Desire Loop

Here is the click psychology. Scrolling, the eye catches a thumbnail and locks before reading anything. Then it drops to the title, hunting for keywords that trigger a desire loop — solve a pain, FOMO about missing the solution, or a clear benefit. Then it returns to the thumbnail, which must amplify that desire, not repeat the title’s words — it is a second canvas to raise want. Kallaway ▶

19.4 Clickbait, not bait-and-switch

You want clickbait — bait the click, then give them the bait. What you never do is switch: promise one thing and deliver another. The intro’s job is to confirm the desire loop in the first five seconds, so the click never feels betrayed. Kallaway ▶

19.5 The rocket-booster model

Packaging has exactly one job — get the click — and then it is discarded, like a rocket booster falling away once the rocket is up. The intro confirms the click (first ~5 seconds) and carries the viewer to the first dopamine hit (within 30–60 seconds); by then the title and thumbnail are forgotten. Kallaway ▶

Chapter 20. YouTube: Analytics, the Algorithm & Evergreen

20.1 The 1-of-10 ranking (CTR is noisy)

Callaway runs mostly on intuition, not retention charts, but the one YouTube-Studio metric he trusts is the 1-of-10 relative ranking — where this video’s views rank against your last ten. A 1–3 is outperforming (study it and replicate); a 4–6 is usually just a smaller-TAM idea; a 7–10 gets the one lever you can pull in real time — repackage the title and thumbnail. Kallaway ▶ CTR is noisy on its own (a high CTR can still flop), and for a business the real metric is dollars-per-view, not views (§13.6). Kallaway ▶

Reading the 1-of-10 rank
1–3 replicate  ·  4–6 smaller TAM, let it ride  ·  7–10 repackage the title/thumbnail

How the reach itself builds is the same sample-pool cascade as short form (§11.7): transcribe → topic-map → seed ~200 → boost 10× on a strong signal, throttle on a weak one — with the extra long-form gate that they must click the thumbnail to even start. Kallaway ▶

20.2 Evergreen: every video is a 24/7 sales agent

YouTube is evergreen (unless the content is news-based and time-sensitive), which changes what a video is. If your content is aligned with your offer, every video is a sales agent working 24/7 — reactivatable at any moment and juiced to buyers for free. Shorts don’t behave this way; their half-life is 72 hours to five days. Kallaway ▶ Two evergreen moves: revive a dead video by re-titling and repackaging it (videos left for dead have gone big nine months later on a new title), and if you are re-uploading an old cut, delete the original and wait ~3 days to sever YouTube’s copyright memory so the re-upload counts as fresh. Kallaway ▶

20.3 Expectations: three to five years, one rep a week

If you are a business, you are not a YouTuber — you are an entrepreneur using YouTube as a free traffic source, so treat it like any paid channel: iterate with data, don’t quit on one rep. Kallaway ▶ But set the clock honestly: don’t start unless you will put three to five years in, because each rep takes five to seven days and the learning loop is far slower than short form. You don’t need many views — a few thousand on-target views is huge — but you do need many more reps than you think. Kallaway ▶
